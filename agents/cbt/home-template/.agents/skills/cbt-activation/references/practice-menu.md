# Activation practice menu

The first eight entries summarize Burns' chapter 5. Select rather than prescribe the entire list.

| Method | Procedure | Useful observation |
|---|---|---|
| Daily schedule | Write a few realistic planned blocks; later record what happened, including rest | Gaps are information, not failures |
| Pleasure and mastery | Notice enjoyment and a sense of doing/coping separately | A low-pleasure necessary task may still have value |
| Anti-procrastination sheet | Pick an avoided task; predict difficulty/reward; attempt a small part; compare | Does anticipation overstate the cost or understate reward? |
| Pleasure prediction | Predict enjoyment for a chosen activity, alone or with others, then observe | Test dependency assumptions without requiring a particular result |
| Task-interfering/helping thoughts | Pair the specific obstacle thought with a credible enabling response | “I can do one part” can be sufficient |
| Small steps/time limits | Break a task into an observable first action and a short bounded attempt | Stop at the agreed limit if needed |
| Choice instead of coercion | Translate “I must” into reasons, tradeoffs and a chosen action | Explore ambivalence without moralizing |
| Count attempts/accomplishments | Record small concrete actions and effort, not just remaining work | Avoid turning tallying into another perfectionistic demand |

Additional chapter-5 options: rehearse a manageable start; list the benefits and costs of acting versus avoiding; respond to external pressure without surrendering choice; identify what can be learned even if an attempt is imperfect. Imagining success supports rehearsal, not magical certainty or guaranteed outcomes.

For avoidance, identify what happens immediately after not doing the task and what happens later. Choose an alternative that reduces avoidance while respecting actual danger and physical capacity. CCI provides an additional activity/enjoyment/achievement framework; use an original simple worksheet rather than copying a proprietary form.

If nothing seems possible, reduce the step, address basic care or companionship, or encourage appropriate professional support for sustained impairment. More ambitious homework is not the automatic answer.
